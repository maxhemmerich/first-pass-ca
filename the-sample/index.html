What the 141 postings are, and what they are not

Every figure this site prints comes from one saved set of job postings rather than from a survey. This page is that set, described plainly — the window it was gathered in, the places and the roles inside it, what the postings asked and what they published — and then the part worth reading before spending anything: what a set this shape supports, and where its reach stops.

02

The window, and where the postings were

The whole set was gathered between 24 September and 5 October 2026, saved as the applications went out. It is one job seeker's pipeline through that stretch and nothing wider. Geographically it leans hard to Ontario — the locations the set names are Toronto, Mississauga, Markham, Oakville and Burlington — which is one applicant's reach, not a map of where Canadian technical work sits.

The roles cluster into six families: data analyst, business intelligence, data engineering, machine learning, clinical data, and software. The weight sits on analytics, business intelligence, clinical data and operations analysis — the families this applicant was actually applying into.

03

What the postings asked about

43 of the postings exposed the employer's own screening questions, so those 43 could be counted instead of guessed at. Pay is the most common question by a wide margin: 27 of the 43 ask something about money, and they ask it 20 different ways. Work authorization or sponsorship is next, written into 12 of the 43, in variants that run from eligible to authorized to require sponsorship. The list then narrows — years of experience (10), a completed degree (6), technologies named outright (6), location or commute (5), references or a background check (4), accommodation requests (3), French proficiency (2), and start date or availability (1).

Two shapes sit at the extremes and are answered in opposite ways. One posting asks for a grid of 41 skills, every row carrying its own required years and proficiency. Another asks what technologies you know and stops the answer at 200 characters. One demands breadth you can enumerate; the other tests how tightly you can edit a single line.

04

What the postings actually published

Only a minority printed a number at all. Seven postings published an annual band and two published an hourly rate — nine ranges in the set, quoted from the ads rather than computed from them. Most postings in the sample published no band whatsoever, so where one exists it is the exception here, not a market range.

05

What this sample can, and cannot, support

  • Evidence about a set, not a survey. 141 postings is what one applicant applied to inside a twelve-day window. It is not a random draw from Canadian employers, and it describes no employer's internal screening software.
  • Every number is a match. The counts are keyword matches against those posting texts, and the salary ranges are quoted where a posting chose to print one. Nothing on this page is estimated, extrapolated or averaged into a trend.
  • The shape is the limit. Ontario-heavy across six analytics-leaning families, the set speaks to what technical candidates in that one pipeline were asked. It does not carry the weight to describe the Canadian market on its own, and this page says so rather than leaving it to a headline.
  • The breadth is elsewhere, and it is cited. The market-wide figures on this site do not come from these 141 postings. They come from government datasets, each named where it appears: median pay per occupation and province on Canadian tech salaries, the same wage file cut by city on tech salaries by city, the file's mean on average pay, open vacancies by industry on job vacancies by industry and by occupation on tech vacancies by occupation, and the federal projection to 2033 on projected tech openings.
